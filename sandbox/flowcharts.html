How a case is judged — flagged or cleared

One office runs down the detector stack. Each check is a yes/no gate: yes flags it (with the reason) and sends it to an auditor; no falls through to the next check. Pass every check and it's cleared. (Oval = start/end · diamond = a check · box = a step · red = flagged · green = let off.)

Every red stop is a reason, not just a verdict — the office is sent to a human auditor with the specific tell that caught it. The checks run cheapest-first (free rules before the model before the costly audit). On generator-style worlds most clean offices fall straight through — but on realistic worlds, where honest offices hold unspent budget, many trip the retention check and get flagged anyway. That over-accusation is the project's main open problem.

The math, in plain English

Each box is one small idea. Here's the idea, then the formula.

① Making the world

sandbox.html · world.py

Picture the budget as water poured into the top of a pipe network shaped like a family tree. Each box passes most of it down, keeps a little, and a corrupt box secretly siphons some off. Nothing just disappears — every dollar is accounted for:

\[ \text{in} \;=\; \text{skim kept} + \text{retained} + \text{sent down} + \text{delivered} + \text{kicked back} \]

Each bucket is distinct: skim kept is the siphon that stays here; kicked back is the slice of that siphon paid on to a co-conspirator (carved out of the skim, so counted once); retained is money legitimately held; sent down goes to children; delivered reaches the front line. The simulator holds this balance by construction, now checked by a per-node conservation assert in make_run.

A "kickback" sends money back up to whoever helped, making a loop. We just follow the looped money around and around. Each lap it gets smaller, so it dies out fast — same reason this never-ending sum still adds up to a normal number:

\[ 1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \cdots \;=\; 2 \]

② A record you can't fake

chain.py

Every page of the ledger gets a wax seal made from everything on the page plus the seal of the page before it:

\[ \text{seal}_n \;=\; H\big(\text{page}_n,\ \text{seal}_{n-1}\big) \]

Change one number and its seal changes, which breaks the next page's seal, and the next… Crack one and they all crack. So nobody can quietly rewrite the past. \(H\) is a "fingerprint" function that's practically impossible to fake.

②⁺ Agreed by a committee

consensus.py

One treasury writing its own ledger could simply lie. So a panel of independent validators — central bank, audit office, judiciary, civil society — each keep their own copy, and a block is only written if enough of them agree (here N = 5 validators, quorum = 4, so it tolerates 1 dishonest or offline node):

\[ \text{committed} \iff \#\text{votes} \ge \text{quorum} \]

A lone bad validator can't rewrite history: its altered chain fails verification, so the honest majority's copy stays the canonical one — and a forged rewrite gets zero votes. This is what turns a "tamper-proof file" into a decentralised ledger no single party controls. (In this prototype that's five simulated validators in one process, not a real P2P network — the principle, demonstrated.)

②⁺ Every payment, in order

journal.py

Alongside the sealed pages we keep a flat running list: one line per movement of money, each line sealed to the one before it — the same chaining trick, standalone.

The subtle part: a single kickback edge fires many times as money cascades around a loop. An edge on the diagram is not a transaction — each firing is. So the list is written from the money actually moving, not from the picture of who-pays-whom. Nothing is missed and nothing invented:

\[ \text{one line} = \text{one firing}, \qquad \textstyle\sum(\text{into } X) = \text{received}_X \]

A cross-check then confirms the flat list and the sealed pages tell the exact same story.

Seeing vs. knowing

ingest.py

We split the world in two: what an outsider could see (the public money trail) and the secret answer key (who was actually corrupt).

The detector is only allowed to read the public part. We keep the answer key hidden and use it afterward to grade it — like marking a test without letting the student peek at the answers. Ingest mints one transaction per firing and commits them through the committee above.

③a Simple rules

gates.py

Quick yes/no checks on each payment. Was the price way above normal? Did money rush out the moment it came in? Did it go to the boss's cousin?

\[ \text{price} \;>\; \text{normal price} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \text{flag it} \]

If a rule breaks, that's not a guess — it's a receipt. Loud cheating breaks a rule like this; disguised cheating tucks every number under the alarm and breaks an aggregate invariant instead (the cluster and subtree tells below).

③b Who sticks out — and who hides in a crowd

analyzer.py

Compare each office to its neighbours. If everyone keeps about 5% but one keeps 33%, that one stands out. We measure "how much it stands out" so a couple of oddballs can't fool us:

\[ z \;=\; \frac{x - \text{median}}{1.4826 \cdot \text{MAD}} \]

Big \(z\) = suspicious. The catch: if everybody is crooked, "typical" is crooked too and nobody looks odd.

Death by a thousand cuts. A careful thief keeps every single number under the alarm — a tiny invoice padding here, a tiny one there. No one payment trips a rule. So we stop judging offices one at a time and look at them together: a whole cluster of sibling offices each padding a little is itself the tell, even when none would be flagged alone.

\[ \text{many small deviations under one parent} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{flag the cluster} \]

③b⁺ Following the money

laundering.py

Draw every payment as an arrow between offices — a graph. Two laundering tricks show up as shapes in that graph.

Round-trip: money leaves an office and finds its way back to it — a loop. We hunt for cycles:

\[ A \to B \to \cdots \to A \]

Layering: a long chain of shells that each instantly forward ~everything and keep nothing — pure relays stacked to blur the origin. We flag the chain.

③c Learning from examples

features.py · ml.py

Show the computer thousands of solved cases and let it find the pattern. It turns each office into a list of numbers \(x\), then outputs a chance it's corrupt:

\[ P(\text{corrupt}) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(w\cdot x \,+\, b)}} \]

That \(\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}\) is just a smooth "S" curve that squeezes any score into a 0–100% chance. For tricks it has never seen, it also asks "how weird is this versus honest offices?" and flags the far-out ones.

③d Checking real life

oracle.py

Some theft looks perfectly fine on paper. So you send a real inspector: was the school actually built? Does the cash add up? The lie shows up as a gap:

\[ \text{gap} \;=\; (\text{what's really there}) - (\text{what the books explain}) \]

Inspectors cost money, so you can't check everyone — you spend them on the biggest cases that look fishy but aren't proven yet.

The same inspector also catches phantom goods (paid for, never delivered) and the fake investor — "investment returns" that really trace back to a connected party or are impossibly high.

④ The report card

sniff.py

Two scores. Precision: when it yells "corrupt!", how often is it right (don't accuse the innocent). Recall: out of all the real crooks, how many did it catch.

\[ \text{precision} = \frac{\text{crooks caught}}{\text{total accusations}} \] \[ \text{recall} = \frac{\text{crooks caught}}{\text{total crooks}} \]

You can't push both to 100% — that's the old idealism. At a fixed false-alarm budget \(f\), with fraud prevalence \(\pi\) and recall \(r\), precision is pinned by arithmetic:

\[ \text{precision} \;=\; \frac{r\pi}{r\pi + f(1-\pi)} \]

With fraud rare (\(\pi\) small), even a tiny \(f\) drowns the true hits. So the honest headline is precision@\(k\) — of the \(k\) offices auditors can actually inspect, how many are really corrupt.

④⁺ Grading at scale — and checking the grade is real

eval.py

sniff.py grades one world; eval.py grades the detector across many worlds, and asks the harder question: is a good score real, or is the test leaking the answer? Several guards:

Shuffle the answer key. Scramble the training labels, retrain, then test against the real ones — averaged over many shuffles. If the detector still scores well it was reading a leak, not learning fraud. An honest mean lands at a coin-flip:

\[ \overline{\text{AUC}}_{\text{shuffled}} \;\approx\; 0.5 \]

Split by world, never by office. Train on some worlds, test on others — so it can't just memorise offices it has already seen.

Hold out a whole kind of fraud. Train on two scheme families, test on the third it never saw — the real test of catching the unseen. A hard lesson: our generators had leaked structure into the labels (a thief was always a leaf in one family, always an internal node in another), so a feature could mean opposite things across families — a confound in our own simulator, not a fact about real fraud. We fixed the generators/features and report all three families, never just the best.

And we watch PR-AUC and the false-alarm rate, not accuracy — when only 1 in 7 offices is crooked, a detector that flags nobody is "86% accurate" and useless.

Recall at a false-alarm budget — the number that actually matters. A score can read ROC 0.76 while the catch-rate at a usable 5%-false-alarm setting is zero. So we fix a budget and ask how many thieves we catch there, measuring the budget on fully honest worlds (no fraud planted) — the real false-alarm rate, not the flattering one measured where thieves are present.

So instead of one recall number we report a coverage matrix (coverage.py): for each kind of theft, which layer catches it — each cell is that layer's recall on worlds of that type (✓ ≥ 85%, ~ 25–85%, · < 25%). Read it with two caveats up front: we wrote the rules, the generators and the oracle, so every row was built to have a catcher — this shows the division of labour, not an unbiased score. And the oracle reads the simulator's ground truth directly — so "phantom ✓" is true by construction, and in reality it (like every layer) would need an inspection budget.

Rules catch structural tells, the ML catches relative retention, the oracle catches what leaves no on-ledger trace. The union is complete except against mimicry (69%) — and the honest row is the last one: bred mimics the breeder invented with no rule written for them (synthetic variants of real cases — e-KTP, laundering, ghost projects — not real data). There the detector gets ~64% recall at a fixed 10% false-alarm rate, versus 17% for hold_rank alone and 11% for random. So it's real transfer, clearly above trivial — but ~64%, not the 99% an unbounded false-alarm rate would suggest.

The false-alarm rate the matrix needs. A union of layers can only add false alarms (\(\text{FPR}_\cup \le \sum \text{FPR}\)). On generator-style honest worlds the union sits near the ML's calibrated ~10% (rules and oracle add almost nothing there); on realistic honest worlds — offices that legitimately hold unspent budget — it jumps to 37% (the oracle stays clean at 2%; the ML drives the rest). The detector can't tell honest holding from a small skim without a delivering baseline.

What 37% does to an auditor (precision@\(k\)). At recall \(r=0.99\) and false-alarm \(f=0.37\), precision \(=\frac{r\pi}{r\pi+f(1-\pi)}\): at 1-in-7 corrupt it's \(\approx 0.31\); at 1-in-100 it's \(\approx 0.03\). That collapse — honest offices over-accused on realistic worlds — not a bigger recall number, is the main open problem in this project.

④⁺ The false-alarm problem — investigated

openproblem.py

The 37% got three experiments. They came back honest — and mostly negative, which is the finding.

1. It's an assumption, not a constant. False alarms climb with how much legitimate holding you simulate — 10% → 44% as the share of honest offices that keep reserves goes 0 → 50%. The "37%" is one point on that curve (near a 40% holding share), so it belongs reported as a curve.

2. The obvious fix failed. Ranking retention within function (ministries vs clinics) didn't help — ranking a group of all-reservers still spreads them 0–1, so the top reservers still flag. The real fix would be deviation from a function's expected hold, not a rank.

3. The deep one — the model misranks. With an inspection budget (ML ranks, oracle confirms the top \(k\)), precision@5% ≈ 0.04 and recall ≈ 1% — worse than random, because legitimate reservers outrank the real thieves, who often steal with low retention. The retention signal is anti-correlated with catching non-retention crime — which is most real crime.

So the open problem isn't a threshold to tune. The honest directions: a per-function expected-hold baseline, or demote retention and let the rules and oracle lead the ranking.

④⁺ The report — and the model explaining itself

report.py · sandbox.html

The scorecard says how well; the report says what and why. For each office it lists the money that moved, the model's chance it's corrupt, the rules it tripped (with the reason each gives), and — the new part — why the model thinks so.

A logistic model's push toward "corrupt" from each clue is its weight times the clue's value, standardised — a log-odds contribution relative to the average node. Rank them and the top few are the explanation, read straight off the trained model:

\[ \text{push}_j \;=\; w_j \cdot \frac{x_j - \mu_j}{\sigma_j} \]

So a vendor reads back as why: hold_ratio, hold_rank, markup — the tells a human auditor would point at. Two honest caveats: these reasons describe the model, not the fraud — if it leans on a leak it will explain the leak confidently; and the Isolation Forest's novelty flags (the open-world case, the ones that matter most) come with no reasons at all. The sandbox shows a lighter rule-layer version; the model reasoning runs in Python.

Two choices keep the shipped report honest: it trains on honest worlds too, so it stops flagging legitimate-but-odd offices; and it calls an office corrupt only above a threshold calibrated to a 10% false-alarm budget on clean worlds, not an arbitrary one-half. Calibrated on one set of clean worlds and measured on another, the false-alarm rate holds at 0.10 ± 0.01 (5 draws) on generator-style honest worlds. Honest caveat: on realistic honest worlds — offices that legitimately hold unspent budget — the same threshold blows out to ~37%. The budget holds in-distribution and breaks out of it.

⑤ The cheating bot

schemes.py · adversary.py · breed.py · arena.py

A bot invents fake corruption to try to slip past the detector. It keeps the schemes that got away, mixes and tweaks them (like breeding animals for a trait), and tries again — slowly evolving toward the detector's weak spots.

So the detector and the bot are rivals in a tug-of-war — the cheater maximises value extracted \(E\) minus a penalty \(\lambda D\) for being detected (profit, not mere evasion):

\[ \min_{\text{detector}}\ \ \max_{\text{cheater}}\ \ (E - \lambda D) \]

The sharpest cheater isn't the loud one — it's the mimic: a scheme that looks like ordinary business, every number tucked under the alarm. The bot evolves toward profitable disguise, and that's exactly what forces the detector to learn aggregate tells (clusters, subtree totals) instead of per-payment rules.

⑥ A second domain — procurement integrity

procurement.py · cartel_eval.py · prozorro_window.py · data/amcu_cartels.csv

The first real data (World Bank debarred firms) showed that what the World Bank sanctions is mostly procurement misconduct — not budget-flow retention, but how contracts are won: bidding fraud, collusion, shell networks. So a second domain alongside leakage: tenders with bids, not money down a tree.

The red flags a rigged tender leaves:

\[ \mathrm{CV} = \frac{\sigma_b}{\mu_b},\quad \mathrm{RD} = \frac{b_2 - b_1}{\sigma_{b_{2+}}},\quad \frac{b_1}{\hat{v}} \to 1,\quad \frac{b_2}{b_1} \text{ (cover-bid gap)} \]

Features: single_bid, bid_cv, winner_premium, n_bidders, rel_distance, bid_skew, bid_kurt, bid_ratio, loser_cv. IsolationForest fit on clean tenders only. loser_cv = σ(b₂…)/μ(b₂…) — CV of non-winner bids only; cover-bid losers cluster tightly → low loser_cv.

\[ \mathrm{loser\_cv} = \frac{\sigma(b_{2+})}{\mu(b_{2+})} \]

Real-world validation — Romania (opentender): concordance with DIGIWHIST experts AUC 0.766 (de-circularized, independent signal). Catch-rate against confirmed AMCU-sanctioned bid-rigging: AUC 0.52, recall@FPR10% = 3.4% — effectively a null. Root cause: Romanian cartels used cover-bidding — fake losing bids. Opentender logs only the winner; the cover bid was never recorded. No feature detects a bid that isn't there. Structural ceiling, not a modeling failure.

Ukraine ProZorro — first data with losing bids: ProZorro publishes all bidders including losers. Built a feed puller (prozorro_window.py) and AMCU cartel labels (amcu_cartels.csv: 6 cartels, 19 firms). Cover-bidder pattern confirmed: De Trading (EDRPOU 42699760) bid 110 lots, won 0 — always losing to ТОВ "ТЕК" (Торгова електрична компанія, EDRPOU 36530971). AMCU fined both firms 110M UAH total.

The laundering playbook — and the tell each trick leaves

Real money laundering runs in three stages: placement (get dirty money in), layering (hide the trail), integration (bring it back looking clean). Every trick has to break some invariant the detector watches — that's how we catch tricks nobody hand-coded a rule for.

Round-trip / kickback · layering

Money leaves an office dressed as a "contract" or "loan", bounces through a company, and lands back with whoever sent it. The disguise changes; the destination doesn't.

Tell: a loop in the payment graph — the money returns to its source.

\[ A \to B \to A \]

caught by: laundering.round_trip

Layering chains · layering

Push the money through a line of shell companies that do nothing real — each just takes it in and passes ~all of it straight on. Every extra hop makes the origin harder to see.

Tell: a chain of pure pass-throughs that keep nothing.

\[ \frac{\text{paid out}}{\text{taken in}} \;\approx\; 1 \quad (\text{keeps} \approx 0) \]

Caveat: a legitimate pass-through ministry looks identical — this flags a structure, not proven intent.

caught by: laundering.layering

Structuring / smurfing · placement

Banks must report any transfer over a threshold. So chop one big suspicious transfer into many small ones, each just under the line, so none gets reported.

Tell: a pile of payments clustered just below the reporting threshold.

\[ \$1.08\text{M} \;\longrightarrow\; 12 \times \$90\text{k}\ \ (<\$100\text{k each}) \]

caught by: gates.structuring

Trade mis-invoicing · layering

Move value across a deal by lying about the price: over-invoice (pay a friend too much), under-invoice (sell them something too cheap), or bill for goods that don't exist.

Tell: a price outside the fair band, or an audit finds the goods missing.

\[ \text{price} \notin [\,\text{lo},\,\text{hi}\,] \quad\text{or}\quad \text{goods}_{\text{real}} < \text{goods}_{\text{billed}} \]

caught by: gates.price_band (two-sided) · oracle phantom goods

Disguised skim · death by a thousand cuts

Instead of one obvious theft, spread it thin. Many offices each skim a sliver and pad invoices just under the fair-price band — no loop, no related party, nothing transferred off-tree. Each one, on its own, looks like ordinary business and sails through every single-payment rule.

Tell: not any one office, but a cluster of siblings all deviating the same small way at once.

\[ \text{many } r_i \in (1,\ \text{hi}) \ \text{under one parent} \]

caught by: analyzer.markup_cluster (aggregate)

Integration — the clean cover · integration

Bring the money back as "clean" — fake business revenue, or "investment returns". A real investor's money has a traceable, independent, market-rate source; a launderer's doesn't.

Tell: income from a connected party, or returns too high to be real. An honest investor is cleared.

\[ \text{return} > \text{market}\ \ \text{or}\ \ \text{source} \rightsquigarrow \text{self} \]

caught by: oracle.wealth_reconciliation
